How far is each field from its fundamental limit?

The five problems I'm building toward

Ranked by headroom × how much the world needs it × how sure we are physics allows it.

  1. 1

    Energy-efficient computation

    A logic gate today burns about 10⁴–10⁵ times the Landauer minimum for erasing a bit. Whole systems are further off still: even the most efficient supercomputer spends about 10⁻¹¹ J per FP64 operation. Meanwhile data centres are heading from 415 TWh (2024) to about 945 TWh (2030).

    Why first: it has the biggest headroom of anything with fast-growing demand. Every other field on this list uses more compute to design and run it, so gains here compound.

  2. 2

    Separations and industrial chemistry

    Pulling one thing out of a mixture uses 10–15 % of the world's energy, mostly through distillation, which runs at 5–20 % of its second-law efficiency. Direct air capture uses about 20× its minimum. Ammonia, aluminium, cement and steel each sit about 2× above theirs, at enormous scale.

    Why second: it has the largest share of real energy use with a 2–20× gap, and the physics targets are exact. Water scarcity in South Africa makes it close to home too.

    See the roadmap to the limit
  3. 3

    Energy storage chemistry

    Lithium-ion is close to its own ceiling, at 1.5–2×. The headroom is in new chemistries: lithium–sulfur and lithium–air cells have 9–12× the theoretical energy of today's cells. Electric commuter aircraft need about 1,000 Wh/kg at pack level to replace most short routes, and today's packs manage about 250.

    Why third: the headroom is big and it unlocks aviation and cheap grid storage. It ranks below 1 and 2 because it needs a chemistry change, which has repeatedly taken longer than predicted.

    See the roadmap to the limit
  4. 4

    Materials near theoretical strength

    Graphene's specific strength is about 16× that of the best carbon fibre and about 1,000× that of structural steel. What's missing is length: the record strengths exist only in tiny, defect-free samples.

    Why fourth: the headroom is huge, from 16× to 1,000×, and lighter structures cut energy use everywhere. Launch costs, which are 3,000× above the energy floor, depend partly on it. The gap is defect control at scale, which is less certain to close by 2060.

    See the roadmap to the limit
  5. 5

    Efficient robots for an ageing world

    Legged robots use 3–14× more energy per kilogram per metre than a walking human. Electric motors already beat muscle on power, so the gap is in control, efficient force at low speed, and compliance. By the late 2070s, people over 65 will outnumber children.

    Why fifth: the demand is certain because of demographics, and the gap is real. The benchmark is biology rather than a hard physical limit, though, so it's the least rigorous of the five.
